Axial precession · 25,771-year cycle · Version 3.5

The pole star is temporary.

Earth's axis sweeps a 23.4° circle around the pole of the ecliptic, once every 25,772 years. Whichever star happens to sit near that moving point gets called the north star. Thuban held the title when the pyramids were built; Polaris holds it now; Vega gets it around 13,800 CE.

How this is calculated

The stars don't move; the pole does. This chart is drawn in ecliptic coordinates, the frame precession leaves alone. Each star's J2000 right ascension and declination is rotated into that frame once. The north celestial pole then sits at ecliptic latitude 90° − ε and walks backwards in longitude at 50.2879 arcseconds per year — one lap in 25,772 years.

Check the model against history. Thuban's closest approach comes out at 2833 BCE, 0.24° from the pole — the tightest fit any star gets in the whole cycle. Kochab takes over around 1600 BCE and hands off to Polaris near 500 CE, matching the classical record. Polaris reaches 0.45° in 2102 CE, then starts to fall away.

Proper motion is a linear extrapolation. Real stars follow curved paths through the galaxy, and over 26,000 years a straight line is a rough approximation — especially for nearby fast movers like Sigma Draconis and Eta Cephei. Toggle it off to see the pure geometry of precession.

The timeline is exactly one cycle. It runs from Thuban's first close approach to Thuban's return, 25,771 years later. There's nothing beyond the right edge worth showing — the sky at 24,000 CE is the sky of 1800 BCE. A run stops when it reaches the end of its range; press Play and it starts over from the beginning. Turn on Loop and it wraps around and keeps going instead.

Asterisms. The Big Dipper, Little Dipper, and Cassiopeia's Crown are drawn brighter than the surrounding constellation lines, because those three are how anyone actually finds the pole by eye. A 48° field is the smallest one that fits all three, so turning asterisms on sets the zoom there.

Simplifications. Precession is treated as uniform and the ecliptic as fixed, so planetary precession and nutation are left out. Obliquity is held constant unless you move the slider. None of this changes the picture at chart scale, but the epochs shift by a few decades to a century at the far ends.

Star trails draw 25° of the sky's daily rotation about the pole of that epoch. It's the fastest way to see what "north star" actually means: watch which star sits still while everything wheels around it.
